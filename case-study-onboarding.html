PRODUCT ONBOARDING · UX STRATEGY

Designing role-based onboarding for complex clinical workflows.

I explored how we could move training closer to the product experience by giving users a more relevant, role-based path through onboarding.

My Role

Product / UX Designer

Focus

UX strategy, user flows, interaction design, prototyping

Context

Healthcare · Clinical research technology

Stage

Concept exploration + proof of concept

01 · OVERVIEW

The problem was not a lack of training. It was discoverability.

Our team already had training videos, PDFs, and support resources, but users were not engaging with them as much as the team wanted.

The initial conversation focused on how to drive more traffic to the existing SharePoint training site. I reframed the problem: instead of asking users to find training outside the product, could we bring the right guidance into the experience itself?

PROBLEM REFRAME

“How might we help users understand what they need to learn, based on their role and workflow, without making them search through a separate training repository?”

02 · MY APPROACH

I designed the experience around relevance, progression, and scale.

Because different users perform different tasks, I did not want onboarding to feel like one universal tutorial. I structured the concept around role-based pathways that surface the most important content first.

01

Relevant

Show users the resources that matter most to their role.

02

Contextual

Introduce guidance closer to the workflow instead of forcing users to leave the product.

03

Progressive

Focus first on essential tasks, then introduce deeper guidance as users continue working.

04

Scalable

Support more roles, resources, and workflows without rebuilding the experience from scratch.

03 · DESIGN PROCESS

From training repository to product onboarding strategy.

01

Understand the current experience

I reviewed how users accessed existing training and identified the friction created by leaving the product, navigating a separate repository, and deciding which resources were relevant.

02

Define role-based pathways

I structured onboarding around user roles so each person could receive a focused checklist instead of the full training library.

03

Design the interaction

I explored a Getting Started checklist, onboarding prompts, training links, and contextual guidance that could support users from first login through continued product use.

04

Consider implementation early

A fully custom onboarding system would require engineering investment, so I also explored how the concept could be tested using a lower-effort product-adoption platform.

04 · THE SOLUTION

A guided starting point instead of a library users have to search.

The core experience centers on a role-based Getting Started checklist that gives users a clear sequence and visible progress.

01

Immediate orientation

New users can be introduced to onboarding as they enter the product instead of having to discover it themselves.

02

Role-based learning

Users see the most relevant content for their responsibilities rather than the entire resource library.

03

Visible progress

Checklist completion gives users a clearer sense of what they have completed and what remains.

05 · PROOF OF CONCEPT

Testing the strategy before asking for a larger build.

Once engineering effort became a major consideration, I focused on how we could validate the idea with less implementation cost.

Our team explored Userflow as a way to layer onboarding onto the existing application. I created a proof of concept to test behaviors such as a new-user checklist, welcome flow, training links, product tooltips, and guided walkthroughs.

POC FOCUS

Welcome flow · Role-based checklist · Training links · Tooltips · Product walkthroughs · Restart/reset behavior

The goal was not simply to reproduce the original UI. It was to learn whether the onboarding strategy could work before committing to a more expensive custom implementation.

06 · MEASURING SUCCESS

What I would measure after launch.

Because the work is still in the concept and proof-of-concept stage, I focused on defining the signals that would matter during a pilot rather than presenting unverified results.

Onboarding completion

Are users completing the highest-priority items in their assigned pathway?

Resource engagement

Are users interacting with training more often when it is surfaced contextually?

Time to confidence

Can users reach key workflows with less uncertainty or support?

Support demand

Are common onboarding questions or repeated training requests reduced?

07 · OUTCOME & REFLECTION

The biggest shift was how we thought about the problem.

The project moved the conversation from promoting a training repository to designing onboarding as part of the product experience.

It also reinforced the importance of balancing user needs, business goals, and technical constraints. When a custom build represented significant engineering effort, I explored a smaller proof of concept that could help the team learn before making a larger investment.

THE SHIFT

From: “How do we get users to visit our training site?”

↓

To: “How do we bring the right learning experience to the user?”
