PRODUCT ONBOARDING · UX STRATEGY

Bringing the right training to users instead of asking users to find it.

I designed a role-based onboarding experience for a complex clinical trial match search product, creating a guided path that connects users with the training and workflows most relevant to their role.

My Role

Product / UX Designer

Focus

UX strategy · Information architecture · User flows · Interaction design · Prototyping

Environment

Healthcare · Clinical research technology

Status

Implementation-ready · Awaiting engineering capacity

01 · THE OPPORTUNITY

The training existed. The connection to the product didn't.

Our team had already created training videos, PDFs, and supporting resources, but engagement with the SharePoint training site was low.

The initial question was how to increase traffic to those resources. I reframed the problem around the user experience: instead of asking users to leave the product, find a separate training repository, and determine what applied to them, we could bring relevant learning into the product experience.

DESIGN QUESTION

How might we connect users with the right training at the right time, based on their role and workflow?

02 · MY APPROACH

I shifted the experience from a content library to a guided pathway.

Because clinical users can have different responsibilities, permissions, and workflows, a single onboarding sequence would create unnecessary noise. I structured the experience around role-specific pathways instead.

Each pathway prioritizes the resources and product guidance a user needs to get started while keeping additional learning available when it becomes relevant.

01

Role-based

Prioritize training according to the user's responsibilities instead of presenting the entire library.

02

Contextual

Bring guidance closer to the workflow so users do not have to search for help somewhere else.

03

Progressive

Introduce essential information first and deeper learning as users progress through the product.

04

Scalable

Create a framework that can support additional roles, resources, and workflows over time.

03 · DESIGN PROCESS

From problem framing to an implementation-ready experience.

01

Understand the friction

I examined the existing training experience and the steps required for users to move from the product to SharePoint, locate the right resource, and determine whether it applied to their work.

02

Structure the experience

I mapped onboarding around user roles and organized the experience into focused pathways. This established what users should encounter first, what could be introduced later, and how supporting resources fit into the journey.

03

Design the interaction

I designed a Getting Started checklist as the central onboarding mechanism, supported by training links, product prompts, and contextual guidance. The experience gives users a clear starting point and visible progress.

04

Design for implementation

Engineering capacity was an important constraint, so I evaluated how the experience could be delivered without requiring the team to immediately build a custom onboarding framework from the ground up.

04 · THE SOLUTION

A clear starting point tailored to the user.

The final direction combines role-based learning with guidance inside the product, reducing the burden on users to determine where to begin.

01

Getting Started

New users receive a clear onboarding entry point rather than having to discover training independently.

02

Personalized pathways

Training and guidance are organized around the resources most relevant to each user's role.

03

Progress visibility

A checklist gives users a clear sense of what they have completed and what remains.

05 · IMPLEMENTATION STRATEGY

I carried the design beyond Figma to evaluate how we could ship it.

A strong solution also needed to account for engineering capacity.

Building the entire onboarding framework natively would require engineering time that was not immediately available. Rather than letting implementation constraints stall the work, the team evaluated Userflow as a potential delivery layer for the experience.

I built a working proof of concept to translate the design into realistic product behavior and evaluate how the onboarding model could operate within the existing application.

IMPLEMENTATION POC

New-user welcome flow · Role-based checklist · Training resources · Product tooltips · Guided walkthroughs · Restart and reset behavior

This gave the team a practical path toward implementation while reducing the need to commit immediately to a larger custom build. The experience is prepared for implementation once engineering capacity becomes available.

06 · SUCCESS CRITERIA

Defining what success should look like before launch.

I defined measurement around whether the new experience improves access to training and helps users move through onboarding with greater clarity.

Onboarding completion

Track completion of priority onboarding items within each role-based pathway.

Training engagement

Measure interaction with resources surfaced through the onboarding experience.

Time to confidence

Evaluate whether users can reach important workflows with less uncertainty.

Support dependency

Look for reductions in repeated onboarding questions and reliance on manual training support.

07 · OUTCOME

Ready for implementation when engineering capacity allows.

The work transformed an initial training-engagement problem into a broader product onboarding strategy.

Rather than simply redesigning where training lives, I designed a system for connecting users to relevant learning throughout their product experience.

The role-based structure, core interactions, and implementation approach are defined. The next step is implementation when engineering capacity becomes available, followed by measuring adoption and iterating from real user behavior.

THE STRATEGIC SHIFT

From: “How do we get users to visit our training site?”

↓

To: “How do we bring the right learning experience to the user?”

REFLECTION

Designing the solution meant designing a path to delivery.

This project reinforced that senior-level design decisions extend beyond the interface.

The work required balancing user needs, product goals, content structure, scalability, and engineering constraints. When the ideal implementation required more development capacity than was immediately available, I focused on creating a practical path forward rather than reducing the problem to a smaller UI change.

That shift—from solving the requested problem to identifying the underlying product opportunity—is ultimately what shaped the direction of the experience.
