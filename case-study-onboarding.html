PRODUCT ONBOARDING · UX STRATEGY

Designing a role-based onboarding experience for complex clinical workflows.

I explored how product-led onboarding could help clinical research users discover the training, resources, and workflows most relevant to their role—without requiring a large custom engineering effort.

My Role

Product / UX Designer

Focus

UX strategy, information architecture, user flows, interaction design, prototyping

Context

Healthcare · Clinical research technology

Stage

Concept exploration + proof of concept

01 · OVERVIEW

The challenge wasn't creating more training. It was connecting users to the right training.

Our product team already had a collection of training videos, PDFs, and supporting resources available through SharePoint. The problem was that those resources were not receiving the level of engagement the team wanted.

During a product team discussion, the question initially centered on how we could drive more traffic to the existing training site. I saw an opportunity to look at the problem differently.

Instead of asking users to leave the product, navigate to another system, and determine which resources applied to them, I began exploring how onboarding and contextual guidance could become part of the product experience itself.

THE PROBLEM I REFRAMED

“How might we help users understand what they need to learn, based on their role and workflow, without making them search through a separate training repository?”

This reframing shifted the conversation from promoting a repository to designing a more intentional product adoption experience.

02 · CONTEXT

Different users needed different paths through the product.

The product supports complex clinical research workflows. That means onboarding is not necessarily a single linear journey. A user may need different guidance depending on their responsibilities, permissions, or tasks.

A generic “getting started” experience risked presenting every user with too much information at once. I wanted the experience to prioritize relevance rather than volume.

01

Relevant

Prioritize resources associated with the user's role instead of exposing the entire training library.

02

Contextual

Introduce help closer to the workflow rather than requiring users to independently search for it.

03

Progressive

Guide users through the most important tasks first and allow deeper learning to happen as needed.

04

Scalable

Create a structure that could support additional roles, content, and workflows over time.

03 · DESIGN PROCESS

I moved from a content problem to a product experience strategy.

01

Understand the existing experience

I started by looking at how training was currently being delivered and where the experience created additional work for users.

The existing approach depended heavily on users knowing that the SharePoint repository existed, leaving their current workflow, finding the correct material, and determining whether that content was relevant to their responsibilities.

02

Define the onboarding model

Rather than treating onboarding as one universal tutorial, I structured the concept around role-specific learning paths.

Each pathway could surface a focused checklist containing the highest-priority videos, documents, and product tasks for that type of user.

03

Map the experience

I explored how a new user could move from first login to orientation, required learning, and eventually contextual guidance inside the interface.

This helped separate what needed to happen immediately from what could be introduced progressively as the user began working within the product.

04

Prototype the interaction

I designed a “Getting Started” experience centered around a checklist. Instead of simply linking users to a library, the checklist gives them a clear sequence and visible sense of progress.

I also explored contextual prompts and tooltips that could introduce key areas of the application at the point they become relevant.

05

Evaluate implementation paths

An important constraint emerged early: building a complete onboarding system directly into the product could require significant engineering investment.

Rather than allowing that constraint to stop the concept, we explored whether a product-adoption platform could provide a faster way to validate the experience.

04 · EXPERIENCE MODEL

A role-based system instead of one-size-fits-all onboarding.

The underlying model separates training content from the user's pathway. This allows the organization to maintain a centralized library while presenting a curated experience to each user.

1 User role

Identify the user's responsibilities or onboarding pathway.

↓
2 Curated checklist

Surface the highest-priority learning content for that role.

↓
3 Product guidance

Introduce important features and workflows contextually.

↓
4 Continued learning

Keep supporting resources available after onboarding ends.

05 · THE EXPERIENCE

Giving new users a clear place to start.

The Getting Started checklist became the entry point for the onboarding experience.

01

Immediate orientation

For a new user, the checklist can appear shortly after entering the product so they do not have to discover onboarding on their own.

02

Visible progress

Checklist completion turns a collection of training materials into a structured journey and provides users with a clearer sense of what remains.

03

Mixed learning formats

The pathway can include PDFs, videos, product walkthroughs, and contextual guidance rather than relying on a single content format.

06 · PROOF OF CONCEPT

Validating the strategy without waiting for a full product build.

Once engineering effort became a key consideration, I shifted from thinking only about the ideal end-state experience to considering how we could test the strategy more quickly.

Our team explored Userflow as a potential way to layer onboarding experiences onto the existing application.

I created a proof-of-concept environment where I could test behaviors such as automatically recognizing a new user's arrival, displaying a Getting Started checklist, collecting onboarding information, and guiding users toward important product areas.

POC COMPONENTS

Welcome experience · User information capture · Role-specific checklist · Training links · Tooltips · Product walkthroughs · Checklist restart/reset behavior

The goal of the POC was not simply to reproduce my original UI. It was to evaluate whether the onboarding strategy could work within a lower-engineering implementation model before committing to a larger product investment.

07 · DESIGNING FOR SCALE

I also considered the people maintaining the experience.

A scalable onboarding system needs more than a polished learner-facing interface.

01

Manage content

Administrators could add, edit, organize, or retire videos, PDFs, and other training resources.

02

Create roles

Teams could define different user roles or learning profiles as the product and organization evolve.

03

Assign pathways

Content could be assembled into targeted learning paths rather than requiring duplicate training libraries.

04

Maintain experiences

Product teams could update onboarding flows as workflows, features, and training requirements change.

08 · MEASURING SUCCESS

What I would measure after implementation.

Because this work is still in the concept and proof-of-concept stage, I would not use adoption metrics as if the experience has already launched.

Instead, I defined the signals I would want to measure once the experience is piloted.

Onboarding completion

Are users completing the highest-priority resources in their assigned pathway?

Resource engagement

Does contextual onboarding increase interaction with training materials compared with relying on the repository alone?

Time to confidence

Can new users reach important workflows with less uncertainty or support?

Support signals

Are common onboarding questions or repeated training requests reduced?

09 · OUTCOME

The value of the project was bigger than the checklist.

This exploration created a path for the team to think about onboarding as part of the product experience rather than as a separate collection of training materials.

It also gave us a lower-cost way to explore the concept before asking engineering to invest in a custom onboarding framework.

THE SHIFT

From: “How do we get users to visit our training site?”

↓

To: “How do we bring the right learning experience to the user?”

10 · REFLECTION

What this project reinforced for me.

Good product design does not always start with a new feature. Sometimes it begins by reframing the problem the team is trying to solve.

In this project, the initial request centered around increasing engagement with an existing training resource. By stepping back, I was able to explore a broader experience strategy: role-based onboarding, contextual learning, and a scalable structure that could connect product usage and training more intentionally.

It also reinforced the importance of balancing an ideal experience with implementation realities. When a custom build presented a significant engineering cost, I did not treat that as the end of the design conversation. I explored a smaller proof of concept that could help the team learn before making a larger investment.

That combination of user needs, business goals, technical constraints, and validation strategy is the type of problem space I enjoy working in most.
